\begin{abstract}
Spatial and temporal variability of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) was quantified at 51 sampling points (SPs) across 17 horizontal positions and three vertical levels in a dairy barn. Campaign~1 covered summer and autumn 2024 using four analysers; Campaign~2 used 32 top-and-bottom SPs in late autumn and winter. Contemporaneous two-hour medians were assessed using coefficients of variation, block-wise errors, mixed-effects models and variance partitioning. Vertical gradients were detectable, but their practical importance depended on gas and averaging scale. In Campaign~1, temporal effects accounted for 70.4\% of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) variance, 67.6\% of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) variance and 44.8\% of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) variance. Median ventilation rates derived from top, middle (\SI{3.6}{m}) and bottom (\SI{2.6}{m}) concentrations were 865.9, 950.5 and \SI{981.5}{m^3\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}}. Corresponding \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) emissions were 12.61, 12.80 and \SI{12.57}{g\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}}; \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) emissions were 0.972, 0.982 and \SI{0.943}{g\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}}. Long-term spatial averaging therefore limited effects on median emissions, whereas short-term and near-roof variability remained relevant. At least two vertical levels are recommended for representative barn-scale sampling.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} ammonia; carbon dioxide balance; methane; sampling height; sensor placement; temporal aggregation

\section{Introduction}

Dairy housing is an important source of methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) and ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)), and reliable barn-scale measurements are required to evaluate mitigation measures and derive emission factors. In naturally ventilated dairy barns, large and changing openings prevent the continuous identification of fixed air inlets and outlets. Wind, buoyancy, barn geometry, animals and manure surfaces generate heterogeneous airflow and gas fields; a concentration measured at one position may therefore not represent the air carrying pollutants from the building \citep{Calvet2013,Ogink2013,Saha2013}. Spatial error in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) affects ventilation rates calculated by the \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-balance method, while spatial error in \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) or \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) subsequently affects the derived emission.

The VERA protocol provides a technically defensible framework for reducing this uncertainty. For naturally ventilated housing, internal sampling is recommended at least \SI{3}{m} above the floor, at least \SI{2}{m} from openings and at a density of approximately one sampling point per \SI{10}{m} of barn length. For dairy cattle, at least six independent measurement periods of \SI{24}{h} should be distributed across one year. These requirements standardise spatial placement, seasonal coverage and quality assurance, but do not establish the precision of a particular sampling configuration. Indeed, the protocol notes that the required number of measurement points had not been experimentally determined \citep{VERA2018}.

Field studies have demonstrated spatial variability in concentrations and gas ratios, although their coverage and objectives have differed. Non-uniform distributions of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and tracer gases were reported by \citet{Mendes2015}; heterogeneous airflow was associated with spatial \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) patterns by \citet{Fiedler2014}; and \citet{Wu2016} found substantial spatial and temporal variability in \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) around lying cubicles. Horizontal and vertical concentration differences were also observed in open dairy barns \citep{Durso2022,Durso2024,Sahu2023}. Moreover, alternative inside--outside sampling strategies produced different ventilation and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)-emission estimates during nearly one year of monitoring \citep{JankeSampling2020}. These findings show that procedural compliance does not necessarily imply equivalent spatial representativeness for all gases.

Controlled studies provide a mechanistic basis for these observations. Wind-tunnel experiments show that pollutant dispersion depends on atmospheric-boundary-layer characteristics, opening configuration and barn geometry, and that complete internal mixing cannot generally be assumed \citep{Nosek2021}. Vertically distributed concentration sampling reduced errors in directly calculated emissions in a scaled barn \citep{Janke2020}, whereas CFD simulations indicated that the height providing the most representative \(\mathrm{NH_3}/\mathrm{CO_2}\) ratio changed among convection regimes \citep{Doumbia2021}. CFD-informed sensor optimisation has further shown that informative airflow locations depend on wind direction and spatial correlation structure \citep{Chen2026}. Such studies resolve spatial processes efficiently, but necessarily represent a finite set of prescribed boundary conditions.

Existing field studies have generally resolved either temporal or spatial variability in detail, rather than both. Long-term investigations have used comparatively sparse sampling strategies \citep{JankeSampling2020}, while denser measurements have been restricted to selected horizontal transects, vertical profiles, local animal microenvironments or shorter periods \citep{Durso2022,Wu2016,Sahu2023}. To the authors' knowledge, no previous full-scale study has combined barn-wide three-dimensional coverage, minute-scale sampling and extended observations across contrasting seasons. Such measurements are needed to determine whether spatial patterns observed during restricted field campaigns or under controlled wind-tunnel and CFD conditions persist, reverse or diminish during normal barn operation. They also provide a full-scale field benchmark for assessing the practical relevance of model-derived sampling recommendations.

The present study therefore measured \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), together with their pairwise concentration ratios, at 51 internal sampling points arranged across 17 horizontal positions and three vertical levels. Measurements covered contrasting meteorological periods and functional areas of an operational dairy barn. The objective was to quantify how horizontal position, vertical level and temporal aggregation affected gas concentrations and their propagation into \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-balance ventilation and emission estimates. The null hypothesis was that the long-term distributions of the measured gases did not differ among horizontal sampling positions or vertical levels. Statistical evidence was interpreted together with effect magnitude and spatial-versus-temporal variability so that detectable differences were not automatically regarded as operationally important.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Experimental barn and surroundings}

Measurements were conducted at the experimental naturally ventilated dairy barn of the Lehr- und Versuchsanstalt f\"ur Tierzucht und Tierhaltung in Gross Kreutz, Brandenburg, Germany. The facility forms part of the Leibniz Innovation Centre for Sustainable Bioeconomy and is coordinated by the Leibniz Institute for Agricultural Engineering and Bioeconomy. The investigated loose-housing barn was designed for 58 dairy cows and measured \SI{38.88}{m} by \SI{17.65}{m}. Its asymmetrical fibre-cement roof reached \SI{6.20}{m} at the ridge and approximately \SI{3.60}{m} at the sidewalls; the internal air volume was approximately \SI{4529}{m^3}.

The barn contained littered lying cubicles, solid concrete walking and feeding alleys, an automated milking area and a small slatted-floor section. The western alley was cleaned by an automatic manure scraper and the eastern alley by a cleaning robot. The southern gable incorporated the milking-robot room and adjoining structures, whereas gates in the northern gable connected the barn with an exercise yard, however it was closed during entire investiagtion period due to construction work. The surroundings included open land, manure storage, feed storage and other livestock buildings. These structures were relevant because their wakes and intermittent plumes could modify the approach flow and background concentrations.

Natural ventilation through the wall was the principal ventilation mechanism. There were no ridge openings in the barn. Internal circulation fans and an experimental cooling and air-distribution system were present during parts of Campaign~1, but their complete operating history was unavailable. They were therefore treated as contextual features rather than controlled experimental factors. Manure removal, bedding, feeding, cow movement, fan operation and water spraying could generate short-term changes in sources or transport. The sampling network consequently covered the principal functional zones rather than being restricted to a geometrically uniform grid.

\subsection{Spatial sampling design}

Fifty-one individual polytetrafluoroethylene sampling lines were installed inside the barn. Each line endpoint is termed a sampling point (SP). Seventeen horizontal positions covered lying-cubicle rows, feeding and walking areas, manure-handling zones, the vicinity of the milking installation and the open central barn airspace (Fig.~\ref{fig:layout}). Three vertically aligned SPs were installed at each horizontal position. The top SP was located \SI{0.60}{m} below the local sloping roof, the middle SP was \SI{3.60}{m} above the floor and the bottom SP was \SI{2.60}{m} above the floor. The top layer therefore followed roof elevation rather than representing one absolute height. The bottom height was the lowest practicable installation that protected tubes from cattle and retained clearance for tractors and routine barn operations. The middle layer represented the commonly accessible upper barn airspace, while the near-roof layer deliberately examined a region rarely resolved in occupied-barn field measurements.

SPs were numbered as vertical triplets. At each horizontal position, the first number denoted the top, the second the middle and the third the bottom SP. Hence, top SPs were 1, 4, 7, \ldots, 49; middle SPs were 2, 5, 8, \ldots, 50; and bottom SPs were 3, 6, 9, \ldots, 51. A separate sampling line was positioned outside the south-western side of the barn to characterise background concentrations.

\subsection{Gas analysers and multiplexer allocation}

Gas concentrations were measured with two Fourier-transform infrared spectrometers (FTIR1 and FTIR2; Gasmet CX4000, Gasmet Technologies Oy, Germany) and two cavity ring-down spectrometers (CRDS8 and CRDS9; Picarro G2508 and G2509, Picarro Inc., Santa Clara, California, USA). The analysers quantified \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and water vapour. The CRDS instruments were each coupled to a 16-port multiposition valve. The FTIR instruments were connected to their respective multiplexed sampling channels. During Campaign~1, the 51 internal lines were divided among the four analysers: CRDS9 measured SP1--SP16, FTIR2 SP17--SP26, CRDS8 SP27--SP42 and FTIR1 SP43--SP51. The outside line was measured by FTIR1. An analyser-to-SP allocation table is provided in the Supplementary material so that spatial patterns can be audited for possible analyser assignment effects.

\subsection{Sampling sequence and temporal alignment}

The target residence time at each valve position was \SI{240}{s}. FTIR observations were exported as one value per sampling step. CRDS instruments recorded approximately one observation per second. For CRDS data, the first \SI{60}{s} following a valve change were discarded digitally to represent line flushing, and the remaining \SI{180}{s} were averaged. Each resulting record was stamped at the end of the sampling step. The cleaned analyser tables were then harmonised to the columns date--time, analyser, sampling port, SP, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), and were appended row-wise.

Because four instruments sampled different parts of the network in parallel but each instrument switched sequentially among its assigned lines, the 51-point field was not observed instantaneously. A complete traversal of the analyser allocations lasted less than two hours. Two-hour blocks were therefore used when a contemporaneous whole-network summary was required. This blocking limits, but cannot eliminate, confounding between spatial differences and short-term changes in wind, animal activity or management.

\subsection{Campaigns}

Campaign~1 was the primary high-density experiment. Measurements covered summer 2024 and an autumn period from 1 to 24 October 2024. The two CRDS and two FTIR analysers were used to obtain the complete 51-SP configuration. Campaign~2 covered 16 November to 31 December 2024. Technical defects in the FTIR instruments prevented continuation of the four-analyser network. The sampling configuration was therefore adapted to the 32 available CRDS ports. Middle SPs were removed and the remaining top and bottom lines, were reassigned sequentially to CRDS8 and CRDS9. Campaign~2 was thus a seasonal extension of the top-and-bottom network.

\subsection{Instrument harmonisation and quality assurance}

Before Campaign~1, all four analysers were operated for 24 h at a common position in the centre of the barn. Two PTFE lines measured at the same suction location. One line was divided between the CRDS instruments and the other between the FTIR instruments. Relative to CRDS, the FTIR instruments reported concentrations approximately 6\% higher for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), and 9\% higher for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). Campaign~1 FTIR concentrations were therefore harmonised to the CRDS scale using 1.06 correction factors for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and 1.09 \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). Before and after the field deployment, the analysers were additionally tested with test-gas concentrations of \SI{700}{ppm} \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \SI{7}{ppm} \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and \SI{5}{ppm} \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). These test cylinder checks were treated as analyser accuracy checks.

\subsection{Meteorological context}

Hourly air temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, wind speed and wind direction were obtained from the German Meteorological Service (DWD) for the nearest suitable station and aligned with the concentration records. These data described the seasonal and synoptic context of the two campaigns. They were not interpreted as direct measurements of velocity or flow direction at the barn openings. Consequently, meteorological comparisons were used to support interpretation of changing dispersion conditions, not to assign individual SP differences to a unique ventilation pathway.

\subsection{Data processing and temporal aggregation}

The cleaned analyser records were retained at their native campaign resolution and then grouped into clock-aligned two-hour blocks for contemporaneous network comparisons. A median was calculated for every SP and response within each block. Network analyses required at least 80\% of the maximum eligible SP count. Campaign~1 yielded 1,338 candidate blocks, of which 1,010 satisfied the coverage criterion and typically contained all 51 SPs. Campaign~2 yielded 552 candidate blocks, of which 136 contained at least 26 of the 32 eligible CRDS-connected SPs. No observations were removed using an interquartile-range or other statistical outlier filter. Missing, zero and negative concentrations were excluded response-wise.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Concentrations and the dimensionless ratios \(\mathrm{CH_4}/\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}/\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_3}/\mathrm{CH_4}\) were compared across height, horizontal position and time. Arithmetic means, medians, minima, maxima, SDs and CVs described the full network and vertical groups. Robust CV was calculated as \(100(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD})/\mathrm{median}\) because concentration and ratio distributions were skewed.

For each block, a vertical-group median was compared with the contemporaneous full-network median. MdAPE quantified typical absolute disagreement without cancellation of positive and negative deviations. NRMSE gave greater weight to episodic peaks, and the 95th percentile of absolute percentage error described upper-tail risk. Individual SP sensitivity analyses used a sampling-point-excluded network median, calculated from all other eligible SPs in the same block. These secondary rankings are reported in the Annex.

Vertical effects were evaluated with mixed-effects models fitted to log-transformed two-hour medians. Height was a fixed effect, horizontal position an adjustment factor and two-hour block a random intercept. Holm-adjusted contrasts were back-transformed as concentration ratios with 95\% confidence intervals. A second model included random intercepts for time block and SP after adjustment for height; its variance components quantified changing temporal conditions, persistent SP differences and residual variability. Paired Wilcoxon tests and Shannon-entropy diagnostics were retained as secondary sensitivity analyses in the Annex.

\subsection{Standard \texorpdfstring{\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)}{CO2}-balance ventilation and emission calculation}

The propagation of spatial concentration differences into ventilation and emission estimates was evaluated using a standard-input \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-balance calculation. For every two-hour block, the median of the eligible internal SPs was paired with the contemporaneous outside line. Standard animal inputs were fixed at 58 dairy cows of \SI{500}{kg} liveweight, \SI{31.22}{kg\,cow^{-1}\,d^{-1}} milk production and a mean pregnancy day of 119.55. Heat production was calculated as
\begin{equation}
\Phi = 5.6m^{0.75}+22Y+1.6\times10^{-5}p^3,
\end{equation}
where \(m\) is liveweight (kg), \(Y\) is milk production (kg cow\(^{-1}\) d\(^{-1}\)) and \(p\) is pregnancy day. Carbon dioxide production was fixed at \SI{0.185}{m^3\,h^{-1}} per heat-production unit, giving \(P_{\mathrm{CO_2}}=\SI{14.016}{m^3\,h^{-1}}\) for the barn. The ventilation rate was
\begin{equation}
Q=\frac{P_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\,10^6}{C_{\mathrm{CO_2,in}}-C_{\mathrm{CO_2,out}}},
\end{equation}
and pollutant emission was
\begin{equation}
E_g=Q\left(C_{g,\mathrm{in}}-C_{g,\mathrm{out}}\right),
\end{equation}
after conversion of ppm to \si{mg\,m^{-3}} using DWD air temperature and an assumed pressure of \SI{101325}{Pa}. Rates were normalised to one \SI{500}{kg} livestock unit (LU). Annualised rate equivalents were calculated as \(E_g\times8760/1000\). These calculations express persistence of the observed hourly rate and are not seasonally weighted annual inventories.

All calculation rows were retained for auditing. Primary summaries required a positive \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) enhancement of at least \SI{200}{ppm}; \SIrange{70}{199}{ppm} was retained for sensitivity analysis, and non-positive enhancements produced no ventilation estimate. Negative pollutant enhancements were retained in the unfiltered output because they diagnose background-reference mismatch, but were not interpreted as physical negative emissions. Campaign~2 only measured inside 32-SP but not outside concentrations, and was therefore excluded from comparative emission inference.

\subsection{Concentration distributions across sampling points}

The concentration distributions were right-skewed, with the extent of skewness differing among gases and ratios. Across SPs, the median absolute difference between the mean and median of the two-hour summaries was approximately 1.1\% for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) in both campaigns. It was 1.7 and 3.6\% for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), and 4.0 and 3.9\% for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), in Campaigns~1 and 2, respectively. The largest mean--median separation occurred for \(\mathrm{NH_3}/\mathrm{CH_4}\), for which the corresponding median differences were 9.4 and 8.2\%. Nevertheless, mean- and median-based SP rankings were strongly associated (Spearman \(\rho=0.955\)--0.995 across responses and campaigns). Mean~\(\pm\)~SD was therefore retained for descriptive presentation, whereas median-based errors were used to evaluate typical representativeness.

\subsection{Vertical concentration structure}

All planned height contrasts were significant after multiplicity adjustment. In Campaign~1, the estimated top-to-bottom ratios were 1.017 (95\% CI 1.015--1.019) for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), 1.031 (1.026--1.036) for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), and 1.045 (1.039--1.051) for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). The top level was also higher than the middle level, with ratios of 1.010, 1.013 and 1.013, respectively. Middle-to-bottom ratios were 1.007 for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), 1.017 for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), and 1.032 for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). Thus, the estimated ordering was top~\(>\)~middle~\(>\)~bottom for all three gases, although the proportional separation was gas-specific.

The top--bottom contrasts were larger in Campaign~2. Estimated ratios were 1.033 (95\% CI 1.031--1.036) for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), 1.074 (1.064--1.084) for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), and 1.228 (1.219--1.237) for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). The largest vertical separation was therefore consistently observed for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), followed by \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

Despite rejection of the null hypothesis, typical height-group disagreement with the full network was small in Campaign~1. MdAPE was 0.64--1.63\% for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), 1.59--3.93\% for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and 1.77--4.57\% for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). The middle level had the smallest MdAPE and NRMSE for all three gases. \subsection{Temporal variability and aggregation scale}

Spatial variability decreased as observations were aggregated over longer periods, but the extent of attenuation differed by gas. In Campaign~1, the median robust CV across SPs decreased from 7.6\% at the two-hour scale to 3.3\% at the campaign scale for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), from 17.3 to 6.5\% for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), and from 23.3 to 15.6\% for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). In Campaign~2, the corresponding reductions were from 7.1 to 5.5\%, from 22.5 to 16.6\%, and from 24.4 to 23.0\%, respectively. The median spatial range also remained gas-specific. At the two-hour scale it was approximately 31.2, 78.3 and 105.6\% for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) in Campaign~1, and 25.1, 82.2 and 93.5\% in Campaign~2.

Conventional and robust CVs were broadly similar for many SPs but diverged at locations affected by episodic peaks or small ratio denominators. Consequently, CV alone did not identify whether a location was representative of the network: some low-CV SPs retained systematic signed errors, while some more variable SPs closely followed the contemporaneous network series.

Variance partitioning confirmed that changing temporal conditions dominated \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) in Campaign~1, accounting for 70.4 and 67.6\% of their modelled log-scale variance (Table~\ref{tab:variance}). Persistent SP differences accounted for only 4.6 and 6.0\%, respectively. For \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), temporal and persistent spatial components were 44.8 and 25.2\%. In Campaign~2, the persistent SP component increased, particularly for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), for which it accounted for 53.5\% of the variance. Thus, statistically detectable spatial effects were generally smaller than temporal effects in Campaign~1 but became operationally important for winter \(\mathrm{NH_3}\).

\subsection{Spatial and temporal entropy}

Normalised spatial entropy was close to one for all responses, but small systematic differences were resolved. Median values in Campaign~1 were 0.999 for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), 0.996 for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), 0.991 for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), 0.998 for \(\mathrm{CH_4}/\mathrm{CO_2}\), 0.993 for \(\mathrm{NH_3}/\mathrm{CO_2}\), and 0.990 for \(\mathrm{NH_3}/\mathrm{CH_4}\). Temporal entropy varied more strongly among individual SPs than spatial entropy varied among complete blocks. Low temporal-entropy values were concentrated at a small number of SPs, but their identity was response- and campaign-specific. Entropy rankings therefore did not reproduce a single universal representative location and were retained as a complementary description of state occupancy rather than as the sole stability criterion.

\subsection{Ventilation rate and emission estimates}

In Campaign~1, 473 network-median blocks met the primary \(\Delta\mathrm{CO_2}\geq\SI{200}{ppm}\) criterion for ventilation. The median ventilation rate was \SI{960.5}{m^3\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}} (interquartile range 796.9--1099.4), while the arithmetic mean was \SI{943.9}{m^3\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}}. Pollutant enhancements and emissions were finite in 464 of these blocks. Median \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) emission was \SI{12.73}{g\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}} (11.25--14.12), equivalent to \SI{111.5}{kg\,year^{-1}\,LU^{-1}} if the observed rate persisted throughout a year. Median \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) emission was \SI{0.969}{g\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}} (0.729--1.272), corresponding to an annualised rate equivalent of \SI{8.49}{kg\,year^{-1}\,LU^{-1}} (Table~\ref{tab:emissions}).

Sampling configuration affected \(Q\) more strongly than the median pollutant emissions because \(Q\) is inversely proportional to the selected \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) enhancement. Relative to the full 51-SP median, the top-only, middle-only and bottom-only configurations had median absolute ventilation errors of 6.50, 1.74 and 5.14\%, respectively. Retaining the 34 top-and-bottom SPs reduced the median absolute errors to 1.16\% for \(Q\), 1.44\% for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and 2.05\% for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). The Campaign~2-like 32-SP configuration gave corresponding median errors of 1.74, 1.55 and 2.83\%. However, the 95th-percentile error was 7.59\% for \(Q\), 6.31\% for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and 16.06\% for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), showing that agreement in the median did not remove episodic configuration sensitivity.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Near-roof and protocol-relevant sampling heights}

The null hypothesis was rejected because systematic vertical gradients were detected in both campaigns. Their practical magnitude was nevertheless gas- and time-dependent. Campaign~1 top-to-bottom differences were 1.7\% for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), 3.1\% for \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and 4.5\% for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\); Campaign~2 differences were 3.3, 7.4 and 22.8\%, respectively. The middle level at \SI{3.6}{m} gave the smallest Campaign~1 MdAPE and NRMSE for all three gases, whereas the top level produced the largest upper-tail errors. Near-roof accumulation was consistent with buoyant transport towards the sloping roof, potentially modified by recirculation and fan or cooling jets. The larger \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) response also indicated that source distribution and reactive loss prevented it from behaving as a passive surrogate for respiratory \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) or enteric \(\mathrm{CH_4}\).

Vertical variability at barn openings was previously reported by \citet{Sahu2023}, while \citet{Durso2024} found gas-specific height effects in an open Mediterranean barn. \citet{Mendes2015} observed that concentrations and pollutant-to-tracer ratios became more stable above the animal zone at selected inlet and outlet profiles. The present 17-profile network extends those studies by resolving commonly selected heights together with the seldom measured near-roof region over a long period. The findings also support the CFD-based caution of \citet{Doumbia2021} that a nominal height near 3 m is not universally representative across flow regimes. A protocol-relevant middle height may therefore approximate a long-term barn median while still missing short-lived roof-level gradients produced under particular ventilation or cooling conditions.

\subsection{Magnitude and attenuation of spatial variability}

The variance decomposition qualified the statistically significant SP differences. Time blocks accounted for 70.4\% of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and 67.6\% of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) variance in Campaign~1, whereas persistent SP effects accounted for only 4.6 and 6.0\%. Spatial persistence was greater for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and became dominant in Campaign~2. Aggregation also reduced robust CV from the two-hour to campaign scale. Hence, the principal result was not that every SP was interchangeable, but that temporal changes dominated much of the long-term \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) record and smoothed many spatial differences.

This scale dependence reconciles the present results with earlier reports of pronounced local variability. Near-cubicle \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) CVs of 58--71\% were reported by \citet{Wu2016}, whereas sampling-line and opening studies showed that spatial configuration can materially alter air-exchange and emission estimates \citep{JankeSampling2020,Saha2013}. Those studies targeted local or outlet-relevant conditions; the present full-network median represents a different spatial estimand. CV, MdAPE and NRMSE should therefore be interpreted together: CV quantifies temporal instability, MdAPE typical disagreement with the network, and NRMSE sensitivity to peaks. Statistical significance alone does not establish an operationally important sampling error.

\subsection{Functional-zone and diurnal patterns}

The diurnal analysis linked spatial patterns to barn function without presuming causation. In Campaign~1, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) were greatest near the milking passage, \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) was also elevated near the manure pit, and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) was greatest in the lying-cubicle zones. Several maxima occurred during the morning interval that included feeding and milking activity, while cubicle-zone \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) also increased late in the evening. These patterns were consistent with respiration and enteric release following animal location, and with manure-related \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) sources, but repeated event responses could not be separated from ventilation changes.

Previous near-source measurements showed that cow occupancy, airflow and management can produce large temporal and spatial \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) variability around lying cubicles \citep{Wu2016}. The functional-zone differences observed here extend that evidence to the upper barn airspace and identify the milking/manure region and lying areas as distinct source-transport environments. However, feeding times alone are insufficient explanatory variables: event-aligned causal analysis would require contemporaneous cow-location, cleaning, fan, cooling and opening-state records. The present profiles should therefore be treated as hotspot hypotheses for a future instrumented campaign rather than proof of individual management effects.

\subsection{Propagation into ventilation and emissions}

The median Campaign~1 ventilation rate of \SI{960}{m^3\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}} was plausible for a naturally ventilated dairy barn but represented a standard-input \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-balance estimate rather than direct flow. The annualised rate equivalents of 111.5 kg \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and 8.49 kg \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) year\(^{-1}\) LU\(^{-1}\) provided scale comparisons, not annual inventories, because seasonal weighting and changing animal or management inputs were not reconstructed. Related long-term work reported ventilation above \SI{870}{m^3\,h^{-1}\,LU^{-1}} at zero wind and a pronounced wind response \citep{Schmithausen2018}. \citet{JankeSampling2020} found sampling-strategy differences of 63--94\% for ventilation and 11--26\% for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) emission. The smaller median errors found here describe internal-network reduction against a common outside line and must not be interpreted as total method uncertainty.

Spatial coverage propagated non-linearly because \(Q\propto1/\Delta\mathrm{CO_2}\) and pollutant emission combines this inverse term with a second spatial enhancement. A top-only network underestimated the full-network median \(Q\) because top \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) was systematically higher, whereas bottom-only sampling tended towards the opposite direction. For \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), spatial errors in the pollutant enhancement were larger and occasionally reinforced the ventilation error, explaining a 95th-percentile error of 16.1\% for the Campaign~2-like 32-SP configuration even though its median error was only 2.83\%. This distinction between typical and upper-tail error is essential for protocols intended to quantify short periods or treatment effects. It also explains why spatially averaged sampling generally performs better than a single central point, as reported for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)- and \(\mathrm{SF_6}\)-based ventilation estimates and by sampling-strategy comparisons in naturally ventilated barns \citep{JankeSampling2020,Mendes2015}.

\subsection{Limitations and measurement implications}

The standard calculation fixed animal inputs and used regional DWD temperature with assumed atmospheric pressure; time-varying animal heat and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) production were not reconstructed. Operating hours of the fans, cooling system and curtains were incompletely documented, as were cleaning events. Cow presence inside the barn, in the exercise area and in individual cubicles was not continuously recorded. Measurements below \SI{2.6}{m} were precluded by the risk of tube damage from animals, cleaning equipment and vehicle movement. The single outside line could be affected by local sources, and Campaign~2 lacked sufficient outside overlap for emission inference. Sequential sampling combined spatial and short-term temporal change, while the absence of matching velocity measurements prevented identification of exhaust-weighted concentrations. The \SI{200}{ppm} \(\Delta\mathrm{CO_2}\) criterion reduced numerical instability but preferentially retained periods with comparatively large indoor enhancement.

For routine monitoring, the findings favour distributed sampling over reliance on one central SP. The middle layer was the closest single-height approximation to the full Campaign~1 network, but its later removal was caused by FTIR failure and the need to fit the design to 32 CRDS ports, not proof that it was unnecessary. The retained top-and-bottom network preserved the vertical contrast and reproduced median full-network ventilation and emissions reasonably well, yet episodic \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) errors remained. Future experiments should pair concentration measurements with velocity sensors at comparable spatial and temporal resolution, resolve whether neighbouring-barn plumes enter under westerly winds, and use CFD or boundary-layer wind-tunnel experiments to translate concentration heterogeneity into outlet-flux weighting. Such measurements are required before a reduced network can be claimed to reproduce emissions rather than concentrations alone.

\section{Conclusions}

The null hypothesis of no vertical or horizontal SP effect was rejected, but the engineering importance of that rejection depended on gas and averaging scale. Temporal change dominated the long-term \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) records in Campaign~1, and spatial averaging limited the effect of network configuration on median ventilation and emission estimates. Spatial persistence and episodic error were greater for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), particularly during Campaign~2. The near-roof region also diverged more strongly during individual periods than was apparent from campaign averages. Long-term agreement must therefore not be interpreted as evidence that short-term concentration fields are homogeneous.

The middle level provided the closest single-height approximation to the complete Campaign~1 network, but a single height could not resolve the observed vertical dynamics. At least two vertically separated sampling levels are recommended when representative barn-scale concentrations are required, especially where fans or cooling systems may redistribute upper-air gas. Validation of a reduced emission-measurement network will additionally require multiple outside references and velocity measurements matched to the concentration measurements in space and time.
