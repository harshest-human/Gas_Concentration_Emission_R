## **2. Literature Review**

**2.1. The Magnitude of Uncertainty in Naturally Ventilated Barns**
Accurate quantification of greenhouse gas (GHG) and ammonia ($NH_3$) emissions from naturally ventilated dairy barns (NVDBs) is highly complex, primarily due to the stochastic nature of wind-driven and buoyancy-driven airflows. Unlike mechanically ventilated systems where airflow rates and exhaust concentrations are easily constrained, NVDBs exhibit massive spatio-temporal variability in air exchange rates (AERs). Consequently, calculating emissions via indirect methods, such as the $CO_2$ mass balance, relies heavily on the assumption of a uniformly mixed airspace—a condition rarely met in practice (Ogink et al., 2013).

The quantitative impact of this false assumption is severe. Calvet et al. (2013) demonstrated that uncertainties in emission measurements from livestock buildings frequently exceed 50%, largely driven by sampling errors. More specifically, Van Buggenhout et al. (2009) proved that relying on arbitrary sampling positions inside a ventilated airspace can result in errors of up to 86% of the actual ventilation rate. Similarly, wind tunnel validations by Janke et al. (2020) revealed that systematic errors in emission estimations can reach +97% when the number and position of sensors are improperly reduced. These massive margins of error highlight that point-source or heuristic sampling strategies are fundamentally inadequate for capturing the true emission profiles of large-scale NVDBs.

**2.2. Quantifying Vertical and Horizontal Spatial Heterogeneity**
Recent literature has conclusively mapped the three-dimensional (3D) heterogeneity of gas concentrations, revealing that proximity to emission sources and structural openings drastically alters local gas mixing ratios. Horizontally, concentrations fluctuate violently; D’Urso et al. (2022) found statistically significant ($p < 0.001$) horizontal gradients influenced by the distance between sampling lines and their proximity to side-wall openings. Corner regions and boundaries near large openings act as highly turbulent mixing zones that dilute target gases unpredictably (Fiedler et al., 2014; Mendes et al., 2014).

Vertically, the stratification is even more pronounced. Doumbia et al. (2021) and D'Urso et al. (2024) highlighted the profound difficulty of establishing a single representative sampling height. For instance, methane ($CH_4$) exhibits significant temporal and spatial pooling directly within the animal occupied zone (AOZ). Wu et al. (2016) measured localized $CH_4$ concentration variations around lying cubicles, noting a spatial coefficient of variation (CV) of up to 24%, driven directly by the localized eructation of resting cows. Because these microclimates act as concentrated emission plumes before vertical mixing occurs, sampling strictly at roof-level or strictly at floor-level mathematically biases the overall whole-barn emission calculation (Saha et al., 2013).

**2.3. Advancements in Sensor Technology and Machine Learning Optimization**
Mapping these complex gradients necessitates high-frequency, highly accurate analytical instrumentation. Photoacoustic spectrometers (PAS), Cavity Ring-Down Spectroscopy (CRDS), and Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometers have become the gold standard, offering part-per-billion (ppb) resolution with negligible interference from livestock dust and volatile organic compounds (Martin et al., 2016; Kamp et al., 2021). However, the deployment of 50+ sampling lines using continuous CRDS/FTIR switching is economically and logistically unfeasible for permanent commercial monitoring (Wang et al., 2016).

To bridge the gap between high-resolution research mapping and practical farm application, the field is pivoting toward Machine Learning (ML). While supervised ML has been successfully applied to predict $CH_4$ emissions based on proxy environmental data (Hempel et al., 2020), its application as a spatial dimensionality-reduction tool remains in its infancy. Recent approaches, such as CFD-informed sensor placement algorithms (Chen et al., 2026), attempt to optimize sensor arrays by identifying sentinel grids with high predictive relevance for the local mean age of air. However, applying ML directly to high-resolution, empirical 3D field data to extract the exact spatial coordinates that reconstruct the "true" mean of a 51-point grid has not been fully realized. Filling this methodological gap is critical for the deployment of scalable, low-cost sensor networks (D'Urso et al., 2023; Janke et al., 2023).

## **4. Discussion**

**4.1. Decoding the 3D Gas Gradients**
The high-resolution empirical mapping executed in this study unequivocally dismantles the "perfectly mixed airspace" assumption historically utilized in NVDB emission calculations. Our 51-location, 17-vertical-triplet grid captured intense spatial stratification, verifying the magnitude of heterogeneity predicted by earlier partial-scale studies. The observed pooling of $CH_4$ within the lower sampling strata directly correlates with the findings of Wu et al. (2016), who quantified the intense microclimates formed by ruminant eructation in the AOZ.

Furthermore, our data indicate that $NH_3$ and $CO_2$ exhibit distinct spatial behaviors driven by their respective generation mechanisms (manure volatilization vs. respiration) and building aerodynamics. The stark contrasts observed between the periphery lines (near the inlets/outlets) and the core AOZ confirm the horizontal variance reported by D'Urso et al. (2022). By harmonizing our FTIR data to the highly precise CRDS baseline over a 24-hour period, we effectively isolated true spatial concentration gradients from instrumental drift, providing a quantified baseline of absolute spatial variance that is rare in current field literature.

**4.2. Machine Learning as a Dimensionality Reduction Tool**
The primary methodological breakthrough of this study is the application of machine learning not merely to predict emissions (as seen in Hempel et al., 2020), but to execute rigorous spatial optimization. Traditional approaches to reducing sampling points rely on heuristic logic—such as arbitrarily placing sensors in the geometric center of the barn or estimating an "average" vertical height (Doumbia et al., 2021). As demonstrated by Janke et al. (2020), these heuristic reductions can artificially inflate emission estimations by up to 97%.

Our ML-driven approach objectively penalized redundant and highly volatile sampling locations (e.g., highly turbulent boundary corners) to identify a minimum subset of sampling coordinates. The algorithm successfully identified that a drastically reduced sensor network can still predict the 51-point "true mean" with exceptional fidelity ($R^2 > 0.90$). This proves that spatial monitoring in NVDBs does not require dozens of sensors; rather, it requires a mathematically optimized configuration of a few sensors. This dimensionality reduction paves the way for the commercial deployment of low-cost wireless sensor networks (Janke et al., 2023), ensuring they are placed in mathematically justified locations rather than arbitrary ones.

**4.3. Empirical Ground-Truthing for Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)**
Beyond immediate field applications, the 51-point dataset generated in this study holds substantial value for the numerical modeling community. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is heavily relied upon to simulate airflow and pollutant dispersion in NVDBs. However, a major limitation in current CFD literature is the representation of the animal occupied zone (AOZ). Modelers frequently simplify the herd as an anisotropic porous medium with velocity-dependent resistance parameters (Doumbia et al., 2021; Nosek et al., 2021).

The accuracy of these simulated porous media is entirely dependent on empirical boundary conditions and real-world validation. The synchronized mapping of $CO_2$, $CH_4$, and $NH_3$ presented here serves as a high-fidelity ground-truth benchmark. Future CFD studies can utilize our specific 3D concentration gradients to calibrate turbulence models ($k-\epsilon$ or Large Eddy Simulations) and validate virtual sensor placement algorithms (Chen et al., 2026), effectively bridging the numerical-empirical divide.

**4.4. Limitations and Future Trajectories**
Despite the methodological rigor, certain limitations inherently bound this study. The use of a multiplexing valve system meant that the 51 locations were sampled sequentially, rather than instantaneously. Given that natural ventilation is driven by rapid, turbulent wind gusts (Koenig et al., 2018), this sequential delay introduces a degree of temporal smearing. To mitigate this, our analysis grouped data into multi-hour blocks; however, capturing sub-minute spatial turbulence was not possible.

Additionally, the assignment of specific analysers to spatial zones, despite rigorous harmonization, means long-term spatial shifts cannot be flawlessly decoupled from localized instrument drift. Future research must address this temporal gap by deploying dense networks of synchronized, factory-calibrated low-cost sensors (D'Urso et al., 2023) directly into the exact spatial coordinates identified by our ML model. This would allow for true, instantaneous 3D snapshots of the barn airspace across diverse seasonal boundaries, ultimately leading to highly accurate, real-time emission inventories.
